The sixth issue

October 1983: The Dragon 64 Arrives, and Readers Discover Stripes

The October 1983 issue cost 60p in Britain and $2.60 in the US. Its cover showed a rider on a winged dragon above a red landscape. Inside were Dragon 64 news, arcade games, commands to learn and faults to untangle. Alongside the excitement came concern for the company’s future.

The editor promises to carry on

Behind the new machines was concern for the company. The editor described finance of up to £2.5 million, through loans or loan guarantees. Sales during the hot summer had fallen below expectations just as the company prepared for Christmas. Tony Clarke was making way for a manager from GEC. The editor promised that, whatever happened to Dragon Data, the magazine would continue while people used the computers and others made software and peripherals for them.

The Dragon 64 meets the British public

The news pages announced the Dragon 64's first public appearance in Britain, at London's PCW show, running from 29 September to 2 October. Already on sale in America, it offered British readers another 32K of memory and an RS232 interface. The company also planned to show nearly 20 new software titles and its disk system. Readers could meet the magazine's team at stand 140. That same month, Dragon Data also promoted the Dragon 32 with a twenty-questions advert in Personal Computer World. You can see it in our advert gallery.

A computer with a voice

JCB Microsystems was preparing software for its speech module: first a program teaching children to tell the time, followed by letters, numbers, counting and spelling. English text-to-speech software was also in development. The module itself had launched in August at £37.95. It plugged into the cartridge port and used five new BASIC commands. According to the report, its speech processor built a voice from 64 basic speech sounds.

The King climbs back

In the Boots sales chart, Mountain stayed at number one. The King rose from fifth to second, with Frogger entering at number three. Other newcomers included Ring of Darkness in ninth place and Cuthbert Goes Walkabout in tenth. Microdeal held seven of the ten places.

Arcade games on test

John Scriven reviewed a batch of action games. In Storm, aliens climb the walls of a pit shown in 3D. It cost £8, like the other Microdeal titles in the review’s price box. His choice for the month’s most original game was Ninja Warrior from Programmer’s Guild, at £8.95. He liked its idea, smooth graphics and sound: jump over boulders or kick them apart, then face fires and pits.

From the magazine to our shelf

Ninja Warrior also appeared in an advert on page 22. Its £8.95 price included postage and packing. The advert offered 16 levels, competition for up to six players, and keyboard or joystick controls. The game also has a place on our shelf.

One character, one number

Brian Lloyd began his Dragon-training lesson with the link between characters and numbers. PRINT CHR$(65) displays the letter A; using 175 gives a blue square. A short program then displays the characters from code 32 to 255, before showing each character beside its number. A simple introduction to the building blocks of games on the screen. We tried it on our machine: an A and a blue square appeared, then all the characters, coloured blocks included, and at the end each character beside its number, up to 255.

Striped paint outside the manual

Reader K A Blackwell sent in seven lines to type. The program steps through values from 1 to 255, changes bytes &HB2 and &HB3, and draws and fills a circle. He reported that this reveals striped fills too. He explained that the normal colour commands change only the lowest two bits of these bytes, while admitting he did not know quite where the stripes came from. We tried the seven lines on our machine, and the stripes appeared: the circle and the background filled with coloured stripes, in different colours on each pass. The listing is on page 53 of the scan.

Not every address is a place to POKE

A reader's short program filled the screen with rubbish and left the computer refusing even NEW. Cathy Hyde identified the problem: it was writing into the memory BASIC uses to manage its work. She advised against writing below &H400, or into the program and variable area above &H1E00. The safe space she pointed to was memory reserved beforehand with CLEAR. A useful lesson for someone just discovering the command.
