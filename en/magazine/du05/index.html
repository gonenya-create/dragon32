What Dragon Owners Were Reading

Dragon User · Issue 5 · September 1983Cover price 60p
The fifth issue

September 1983: The Dragon Lands in America, and Word Processors Put to the Test

The September 1983 issue cost 60p again, with the blue "International edition" sash and a US price of $2.60. On the cover: a spaceship, a planet, a red car and a Dragon computer, with headlines on sound and graphics, a test of word processors and the Dragon in the US. And in the corner, a competition for a £150 speech synthesis kit.

The Dragon lands in America

Last issue we told how the Dragon 64 would reach America before Britain. This time came the full story. Craig Hanna, vice president of a users' group in Los Angeles, interviewed George Merchant of Tano in New Orleans. Tano was 20 years old, with divisions in oil and gas and in equipment for ships, and it was now responsible for making and selling the Dragon 64 in the US. The computer cost $399, and the box held a 160-page manual, a spreadsheet, a mail-merge program and the word processor Telewriter 64.

Tano expected 250,000 computers in the first year. It would not be sold by mail or on the toy shelves: in Merchant's words, the Ataris and Commodores go next to the toys, and the Dragon goes in the electronics department, where people buy a serious computer. Repairs would be done at about 500 Western Union service centres across the country. And a surprise at the end: a Dragon 128, with two 6809E processors, 128K of memory and the OS9 operating system, to be announced in November. That same month Tano also ran an advert in the American magazine Rainbow, and you can see it in our advert gallery.

"Coming soon" = "free drinks tomorrow"

The editor opened in frustration. After long delays, Dragon Data's disk drives had finally arrived, and Sinclair's Microdrive had nearly reached the shops. He wrote that in the computer world "coming soon" is worth about as much as "free drinks tomorrow". The one who gained from the wait was a small company, Premier Microsystems, which sold drives of its own. At the London Computer Fair, the editor said, laughter from its stand drowned out even the arcade games. The joke: Dragon Data was still fighting a problem with its drives that Premier had solved long ago.

The King drops to fifth

In the Boots top ten, four new titles went straight into the first four places, three of them from Dragon Data: Mountain at number one, then Basic Tutorial from Ampalsoft, then Personal Finance and Special Collection 1. The King, number one for two months, dropped to fifth. Microdeal held five places in the chart.

Word processors on test

John Scriven, who went through the adventures last issue, looked this time at "serious" software: word processors, graphics tools and assemblers. He noted that the Dragon drops letters when you type fast. Microdeal's Telewriter came out the best documented, with about 30,000 words of explanation, and also the most expensive, at £49.95. It was written originally for the Tandy computer in the US. We have a guide to Telewriter, and in America it came inside the Dragon 64's box.

A new ninja in the shops

On page 18 there was an advert from Programmer's Guild: the new Ninja Warrior, at £8.95. You guide a ninja past boulders, fire, pitfalls and flaming meteors, rising rank by rank to NINJA GRANDMASTER. You can play Ninja Warrior on our machine today.

Where did the memory go?

A reader had bought a Dragon 32 a month before, typed PRINT MEM, and got 24871. "Should this not be 32768?" he asked, and wondered whether his computer was faulty. Cathy Hyde, who answered the technical questions, explained that the 32K is there, but part of the memory is set aside: for the graphics pages, the cassette, the screen and the variables. And if you don't need graphics, you can have 31015 bytes: type POKE 25,6, then POKE &H600,0 and NEW.

We tried it on our machine and got exactly the same numbers: 24871, and 31015 after the trick. We also saw the price: the program now sits in the graphics memory, and a graphics command such as PCLS wipes it out.

The fast POKE, again

The speed-doubling POKE, 65495, came back for a third time. Readers complained: the magazine advised against it, yet printed programs that use it, and their computers crashed with it. Cathy Hyde explained that the Dragon was designed to run at 0.89 MHz, and the POKE runs it outside its specification. If the computer crashes, it is not faulty; it is simply doing what it was promised to do. She added that software houses who use the POKE without telling buyers are misleading them, and that it is unlikely to harm the computer.

From the post: when the tape won't save

Two readers sent fixes for a problem every Dragon owner knew: a program that saves to tape and won't load back. Brian Beesley from Hampshire plugged the lead into the recorder's DIN socket instead of the microphone socket. In his view the Dragon's signal was too strong for the microphone input. Mark Greenall from Blackpool fitted a 100K variable resistor between the computer and the recorder, and turned it until the recording came out clean. And another reader corrected the magazine: a bug in USR reported in the June issue is no bug at all, if you write the routine number with two digits.

A program to type in

The letters also had a "graphics hint" from Martin Entwistle of Farnworth: seven lines that put numbers straight into the graphics memory, from address 1536, and so show 255 patterns of coloured blocks. We tried it on our machine, and the screen filled with columns of coloured patterns. The listing is on page 5 of the scan.

An end-of-summer issue: a British computer setting out to conquer America, disk drives that finally arrive, and readers who work out for themselves how to make the tape behave.
