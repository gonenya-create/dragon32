What Dragon Owners Were Reading

Dragon User · Issue 7 · November 1983Cover price 60p
The seventh issue

November 1983: The Dragon 64 in the Shops, and The King Back on Top

The November 1983 issue cost 60p in Britain and $2.60 in the US. Its cover opened up disk drives to reveal their insides. Within were a new £225 computer, software for winter, a comparison of two disk systems and readers’ tips on colour and the screen.

The Dragon goes to California

On the first weekend of November the Dragon went to California. At the first West Coast Colour Computer fair, in Pasadena, the Dragon 32 and 64 had pride of place. The editor noted that the show had grown from a users’ initiative, and that Los Angeles and Pasadena had even declared a Colour Computer weekend. He wanted the same enthusiasm from British authorities. The PCW show had already given Dragon owners reasons to be excited: the grey Dragon 64, a Viewdata interface and a Colne Robotics arm.

The Dragon 64 heads for the shops

The news promised the new computer in the shops in November, priced at £225. Its grey case had the same shape and size as the 32’s, with an RS232C interface added. Alongside it was OS-9, intended to open the way to business software. The price of the disk-based system had not been settled as the magazine went to press. Upgrade packages for 32 owners were planned for early the following year, possibly sooner. The company also promoted its Dragon 32 software range in the new hoops advert in our gallery.

News and messages down the telephone line

A new Viewdata interface offered access to Micronet and Prestel. Its display had 40 columns and 24 rows, with upper- and lower-case letters. The software could write messages, print pages and save them to cassette. The interface plugged into the cartridge port and ran automatically. The expected price was about £50, or £120 in a package with a modem.

The King returns to number one

In the Boots sales chart, The King rose from second to first, followed by Frogger and Android Attack. New entries included Dragon Tracks at seven, Morocco Grand Prix at nine and Dragon Data’s Quest at ten.

A space shuttle and a composer on cassette

John Scriven reviewed Microdeal’s Space Shuttle at £8. The mission involved launching, rendezvousing with a satellite, bringing it into the cargo bay and landing. He found that the game encouraged another attempt at its difficult stages, and praised the clear instruments. The Composer cost £15. It accepted melodies through DATA statements and compiled them into machine code for use in other programs. He liked the results, but warned that matching the demonstration tunes took work.

Two drives, two approaches

Keith and Steven Brain compared Dragon Data’s disk system with Cumana’s Delta-based system. Both had a single-sided, double-density drive with 170K of storage. Dragon Data’s system cost £275; the final Cumana package price was still undecided. The reviewers thought DragonDOS could satisfy owners wanting faster storage and retrieval, but preferred Delta for more advanced work. They also pointed out that disks could not be exchanged between the two systems.

From the advert to the jungle

On the back cover, Microdeal promoted five games, including Cuthbert in the Jungle, Cuthbert Goes Digging and Keys of the Wizard. The printed price was £8 per cassette. Cuthbert in the Jungle also has a place on our shelf.

Clearing the screen a row at a time

Roger Alexander sent in a single line to type. Its loop visits addresses 1024 to 1535 and writes 128 into each one. According to the letter, this clears the screen to black, row by row. Changing the value to 175 should make it blue; 143 gives green and 191 red. We tried it on our machine: the screen filled with black row by row, and with 175 and 191 it turned completely blue and red. With 143 it looks like the normal green screen, because it is exactly the same green. The line and colour values are on page 4 of the scan.

Why did the yellow dot turn blue?

In the answers column, Brian Cadge explained a surprising limit of low-resolution graphics. There are 64 by 32 points and nine colours, but each character square can contain only one colour besides black. Draw a yellow dot and then a blue dot in the same square, and both become blue. We tried it on our machine: SET(0,0,2) drew a yellow dot, and after SET(1,1,3) it turned blue, just as he described. His advice was to plan around 32 by 16 character squares, or use high-resolution graphics.
