What Dragon Owners Were Reading

Dragon User · Issue 4 · August 1983Cover price 60p
The fourth issue

August 1983: The Dragon 64 Goes to America First, and Adventures You Can Play Here

The August 1983 issue cost 60p, again with the blue "International edition" sash and a US price of $2.60. On the cover, a man sits deep in thought over a chess game on a Dragon screen. Inside: news of the Dragon 64, a long look at adventure games, a test of three light pens, a club in a holiday town, and why one POKE makes the machine run nearly twice as fast. Here are a few of the highlights.

The Dragon 64: Americans First

The big news story: the United States would get the Dragon 64 before Britain. In the UK it would cost £225 including VAT and reach the shops at the end of August or early September. In the US it would sell for $399, with shipments starting at the beginning of August. Dragon Data's marketing director, Richard Wadman, explained that the American TV circuitry was easier to build. Over there it would simply be called "the Dragon". Apart from the extra 32K of memory, the main difference was an RS232 port. Dragon 32 owners were promised a CPU swap to turn their machines into 64s for under £100. And hopes were high: over 55,000 American Dragons were expected to be sold before Christmas.

The King Still on Top

In the Boots top ten, for the second month running: The King at number one. Straight in at number two came a newcomer, Microdeal's Talking Android Attack, with Salamander's Nightflight third. The reviewer noted that the "talking" was limited to a few words such as "Intruder Alert", but recommended it as fun to play and good value for money.

"And This Review Be Your Guide"

John Scriven remembered his first adventure game, in 1978, on a mainframe at work. He typed RUN at about six in the evening and got to bed at about four the next morning. Then he turned to the new adventures for the Dragon. Dragon Data offered text-only adventures at £7.95 each: Black Sanctum, with a dark monastery full of hooded figures, and Calixto Island, which begins in a professor's study. El Diablero caught his eye because Ken Kalish wrote it. After a week in its desert he had found only a crow and a strange rock, and gave up. Dragon Mountain, at £4.95, was the opposite: he finished it in just over an hour. Microdeal's adventures, such as Williamsburg Adventure, impressed him less. His favourite was Wintersoft's Ring of Darkness, at £10.

You can play four of these adventures on our machine today, and each one has hints here too, for anyone stuck in the desert like the reviewer: Black Sanctum, Calixto Island, El Diablero and Williamsburg Adventure.

Someone from Leeds

The same review had an arcade game too: Bonka, from J Morrison of Leeds, at £7.95. Six floors and ladders: dig a hole, wait for a meanie to fall in, then run over and bonk it on the head. The reviewer called it another good investment. Elsewhere in the issue, Morrison's own advert offered three more games: Droids, Chess and Vultures. All four are on our shelf: Bonka, Droids, Chess and Vultures. A different chess game, Dragon Data's Cyrus Chess, was also in the review: a cartridge at £24.95, from a program that had won the Microcomputer Chess Championship in 1981. Expensive, the reviewer said, but still good value next to a dedicated chess computer.

Three Light Pens

A light pen lets you point straight at the screen. Clive Gifford explained that on the Dragon it simply plugs into a joystick socket, with no special interface, which kept prices below those for other computers. Trojan's pen, at £10, was the best of the three, and came with a matchsticks game you play with the pen. Microdeal's was the dearest at £12 and the least reliable; it actually worked better on a black-and-white TV. Accord Data's pen, at £9.95, was the cheapest, by just 5p.

A Club in a Holiday Town

The clubs page went to Wareham in Dorset, where John Richardson ran the Dragon 32 Users' Club from his shop. The club was only two months old and already had more than 1,200 members. Members on holiday along the south coast were dropping in to try out add-ons and software. Dragon owners, he said, were as friendly as MG drivers who dip their lights when they pass one another. The first 1,000 members joined free; from now on it cost £5 a year. Among the members' favourite games were The King and Champions, both on our shelf.

Twice as Fast

In issue 2 a reader warned that a POKE to address 65495 could harm your processor. This time George Saint explained what it actually does. A chip called the SAM sets the pace for the whole computer, and a POKE to $FFD7 (65495) makes BASIC run nearly twice as fast. A POKE to $FFD6 (65494) brings it back to normal. There is an even faster mode, but in it the keyboard stops working. One important warning: at double speed you cannot save to tape. The recording sounds too high and will not load. And if your machine cannot cope, he suggested swapping the processor for a faster-rated one.

From the Post Bag

Another trick from issue 2 got its answer too. There we told of a reader who hid a copyright message in line 65535, because that line could not be edited or deleted. This month Norman Silver of Kent wrote that it does not work: RENUM can move the line below 64000, and then it can be edited or deleted. And on the technical questions page, Dragon Answers, Dave Rowntree of London sent three questions. Two were about the processor; the third was "Will you marry me?" Cathy Hyde, who answers the questions, replied that she was far too busy with letters to marry anyone, but "I shall certainly keep the offer on file".

A Program to Type In

The Open File section carried Nail and thread by George Smith of Barnsley. In twenty short lines it draws a sailing ship out of straight lines, like a string-and-nail picture, with a round sun above it. The listing is on page 39 of the scan.

A summer issue: a new computer on its way to America, adventures that kept people up until morning, and a club that gathered over 1,200 members in two months.
