What Dragon Owners Were Reading

Dragon User · Issue 8 · December 1983Cover price 60p
The eighth issue

December 1983: More Memory, More Software, but at What Price?

The December 1983 issue cost 60p in Britain and $2.60 in the US. The Dragon 64 reached the test bench, the editor hoped for more software from America, and the reviewer asked whether good games had to be expensive. Inside were also word-processing routines and a small experiment with random numbers.

The editor looks across the Atlantic

The American launch of the Dragon 64 gave the editor reasons for optimism in Britain. A larger market might attract more software writers. Tano, Dragon Data’s US partner, was already considering software and encouraging conversions and new programs. It also planned to help user groups and provide after-sales support. Its mix of games, business tools and educational software appears in the January 1984 sheet in our gallery.

More adventures on the way

Channel 8 was preparing Dragon conversions of all ten Mysterious Adventures. The names mentioned included The Golden Baton, Time Machine and Escape from Pulsar 7. Release was promised for January, at £9.95 each. Richard Shepherd Software was starting with Transylvanian Tower and Super Spy, at £6.50 each.

The King keeps its crown

In the Boots sales chart, The King remained at number one. Android Attack rose to second, while new entry Cuthbert Goes Walkabout reached third. Frogger fell to fourth. Other new entries included Gridrunner, Shark Treasure and Mined Out.

A good game, but what about the price?

John Scriven enjoyed Dragon Data’s Shark Treasure: send divers to collect gold from the seabed while avoiding sharks. He praised its display and fast response, but thought £12.95 would discourage buyers. The Doodle Bug cartridge, listed at £19.95 in the review table, also seemed too expensive. Storm Arrows at £7.95 offered better value in his view: a fast game in which you had to destroy eight hostile arrows.

A 64 that starts as a 32

Keith and Steven Brain tested the Dragon 64. Alongside 64K of memory, it added an RS232 serial port and keyboard autorepeat. The case was grey. On power-up it began in a mode compatible with the Dragon 32, so the review reported that PRINT MEM returned 24871, just as on the older model. This choice was intended to preserve compatibility with existing software. On our machine, which is a Dragon 32, PRINT MEM after power-up also gives 24871.

Not just games: a word processor

Jim Dawson explored writing a word processor. He considered BASIC fast enough for most typing, but slow at moving blocks of text around memory. The article therefore presented three machine-code routines to tackle editing bottlenecks. It was an invitation to build a useful tool beyond games, rather than a promise of a complete word processor in a few lines.

From review and advert to our shelf

Microdeal’s Cuthbert in the Jungle, priced at £8 in the review table, was Scriven’s favourite game that month. He described jumping over logs, pits and alligators, and improving with practice. Juniper Computing’s advert on page 27 listed the same game at £7.95, including VAT and delivery to the UK mainland. These are two prices from different parts of the issue. The game also has a place on our shelf.

How random is a random number?

Ken Reavley suggested a short experiment: switch the computer off and on, then ask twice for a random number up to 200. His letter said the results were 100 followed by 64, with the sequence repeating after another power cycle. To make the selection depend on when the player pressed a key, he suggested continuously calculating numbers and displaying one only on a keypress. We tried it on our machine: after power-up PRINT RND(200) gave 100 and then 64, and after another power-up 100 and 64 again, exactly as in the letter. The command for the experiment is on page 4 of the scan.

Where is the plotting command?

A reader arriving from the ZX81 asked what replaced PLOT. Brian Cadge pointed to PSET: choose the point’s position and optionally its colour. He also explained that the Dragon’s origin is at the top left. PRESET returns the point to the background colour. The syntax is explained on page 73 of the scan.
